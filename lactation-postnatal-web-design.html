The Problem

Why most lactation and postnatal care websites lose the mother who needs them most.

The new mother searching for lactation support is not browsing. She is often in pain, sleep-deprived, and searching with one thumb while the other hand holds a baby. She needs to land on a page that immediately signals warmth, competence, and availability. Most lactation and postnatal care websites fail this test in one of three ways.

The first problem is generic language. The mother with a latch issue and a two-week-old is in a completely different emotional state than the mother managing supply through a return to work at twelve weeks. A new mother recovering from a difficult birth who needs comprehensive postnatal support has different questions than someone booking a single IBCLC consult. When the copy treats every new mother as the same patient, it resonates with none of them. The second problem is buried credentials. IBCLC certification, CLC training, postpartum doula certification, fourth trimester coaching, patients have learned to look for these. If the site doesn’t surface the right credentials immediately, trust is lost before the contact form is ever opened.

The third problem is tone. A mother dealing with pain, low supply, or a baby who isn’t gaining weight is in problem-solving mode, not browsing mode. She will leave the first site that doesn’t immediately show her it understands what she’s going through. The wrong tone at the wrong moment, too clinical, too cheerful, too generic, costs a booking. A site built for this patient needs to feel like someone who has been in the room with exhausted new mothers, warm enough to be trusted, specific enough to signal expertise, and fast enough to read that a sleep-deprived woman with a baby on her chest can absorb it.

The Build

What a website built for a lactation or postnatal care practice actually contains.

Every build starts with copy. For lactation and postnatal care, that means understanding the full range of patients, the mother struggling to establish supply in week one, the mother navigating supply and return-to-work at three months, the mother seeking comprehensive fourth trimester support after a difficult birth, the mother approaching weaning. That balance is designed in deliberately rather than arrived at by accident.

After launch, the site stays current. New services, updated credentials, expanded virtual offerings, ongoing care means the site reflects what the practice actually does, without you managing any of it. You focus on your patients. The site keeps up.

The Background

Why postnatal care sites need a different approach than general maternal health sites.

Simple Rabbit works exclusively with private-pay women’s wellness practices. That focus matters for lactation and postnatal care specifically, because the patient in the fourth trimester presents a challenge that general healthcare web designers rarely account for, she is searching in crisis mode, often from a device with one thumb, and her emotional state makes the first impression unusually decisive. A site that doesn’t account for that loses her in the first ten seconds.

A general medical website template does not know the difference between an IBCLC and a CLC, or why a patient choosing between them needs to understand it. It does not know that a patient booking a comprehensive postnatal support package has fundamentally different needs than a mother booking a single latch assessment. It does not know how to write a contact page that feels immediately warm and available to a woman who is in pain and exhausted at the same time. Lactation and postnatal care sites require a specific attentiveness in the copy, warm, specific, and fast to read, that has to be written with the patient’s exact moment in mind.

I have audited dozens of private-pay women’s wellness sites, including practices in the maternal health and fourth trimester space. The gaps are consistent: copy that treats the postpartum period as a single undifferentiated experience, credential pages that are technically complete but visually buried, contact flows that feel designed for a patient who has all day to fill them out. These are fixable problems, but they require someone who understands what the new mother is actually experiencing when she lands on the page. That specificity is what separates a site that converts from one that just exists.

Common Questions

Common questions from lactation consultants and postnatal care providers.

My patients are searching in a hurry, how does the site account for that?

The site is built around the reality that a new mother searching for lactation support is not in a casual browsing mindset. She is in a problem-solving mindset, often with a baby on her chest and a limited attention window. That shapes everything: the copy leads with the problem she is experiencing before it describes the solution, the page structure is clear enough to scan rather than read linearly, the contact path is simple and warm rather than formal. The site is designed to earn trust and prompt action in a short window, because that is the window you actually have.

Should I have separate pages for lactation support and postnatal recovery?

In most cases, yes. The patients are different. A mother dealing with a latch issue and a two-week-old has a different question than a mother booking a comprehensive fourth trimester support package after a planned c-section. Combining them on one page means writing for no one in particular. Separate service pages also help with search, “IBCLC near me,” “postpartum doula,” and “fourth trimester support” are distinct searches with distinct intent, and individual pages give each one something substantive to rank for. The exact page structure depends on your specific services and how your patients tend to find you, which is something we work through during planning.

I offer virtual visits, how does the site handle that?

Virtual lactation support and remote postnatal care have become a significant part of how many practices operate, and the site reflects that clearly. For some providers, virtual visits extend reach beyond a local market, which changes the SEO strategy. For others, virtual and in-person serve the same geographic area but different patient situations. We work through which applies to your practice during planning, and the copy and page structure are built accordingly, so that a patient who specifically needs remote support can find and understand that option quickly.

How do I get found for “IBCLC near me” searches?

The foundations are built into the site from the start. That means pages structured around the specific terms your patients actually type, “IBCLC near me,” “lactation consultant [city],” “breastfeeding support,” “postpartum doula”, location signals accurate throughout the site, and a Google Business Profile that aligns with what the site says. What I can’t promise is a specific ranking position, because that depends on your market, your competition, and factors outside any web designer’s control. But the site will be built to give search engines something substantive to work with. For a full breakdown of how local SEO foundations work for private-pay practices, there’s a detailed article on the blog.

My patients are often anxious first-time mothers, what tone should the site use?

Warm, grounded, and specific. Not clinical, a new mother who is struggling does not want to feel like she is navigating a medical intake form. Not falsely cheerful, a mother who is in pain or worried about her baby can tell when copy is glossing over the reality of what she is experiencing. What works is tone that acknowledges the difficulty without amplifying it, steady enough to be calming, specific enough to be credible. That calibration happens during the copy phase, and it is one of the more careful parts of writing for the maternal health niche.
