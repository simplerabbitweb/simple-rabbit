# SEO Brief: Web Design for Aesthetics and Laser Practices

## The buyer

A practice owner running a med spa, aesthetics clinic, or laser-focused practice. Most often a woman, 30 to 55, often an NP or RN injector who launched her own practice, sometimes an MD (dermatologist or plastic surgeon) running a cosmetic-focused clinic, sometimes an aesthetician who's expanded into laser and injectables under a medical director. She's a hybrid: part medical practitioner, part boutique business owner. She thinks about her practice the way a high-end retailer thinks about a brand, and she's operating in the most marketing-aware corner of women's wellness.

This is the most marketing-savvy buyer of the four industry pages. She's been pitched by agencies before, probably more than once. She runs Instagram ads or has run them. She's been quoted $7,500 to $25,000 for med spa websites by big aesthetics agencies (Intrepy, ClinicGrower, Aesthetic Brand Marketing). She knows what a "lead funnel" is. She uses words like "conversion" and "booking" without flinching. She's faster than the menopause buyer to detect a generic pitch and faster than the functional medicine buyer to walk away from one.

What she values: visual quality (the page itself has to look good, not just claim to make good websites), specificity to her niche, and proof that the designer understands what *her* patients search for. She knows her business is local. She knows "Botox near me" is the highest-stakes search query in her world, and she'll judge any designer pitch partly on whether you mention things like that.

Her stage is most often B (recently opened her own practice, 0 to 24 months in) or C (established 2+ years, ready to rebrand). Stage A is rarer here than in the other niches because aesthetics practitioners typically launch their own thing rather than transition out of insurance.

## Primary target keyword

**"web design for med spa"** (and the closely related "med spa website design" and "med spa website designer").

Volume note: medium to high. This is the highest-volume primary keyword across the four industry pages. The flip side is that the SERP is more crowded than any of the other niches. There are real competitors with real budgets and real domain authority in this space.

Settled vs. educated guess: It's settled that this phrase has a substantial existing competitive set (Mila Design, Intrepy, ClinicGrower, Aesthetic Brand Marketing, BOWO Creative all rank for variations of it). It's an educated guess that Simple Rabbit can break into the first or second page within 6 to 12 months because the differentiator is voice, craft, and the fact that there's a published case study to anchor the page. Realistic expectation: top 5 in 9 to 12 months with the supporting content ecosystem in place. This is a longer ranking timeline than the menopause page.

A note on terminology: "med spa" gets more searches than "medical aesthetics" or "aesthetic practice" or "laser clinic." The page should anchor on "med spa" but use "aesthetics and laser" (Simple Rabbit's existing category language) and "medical aesthetics" interchangeably. The H1 should use the phrasing that already shows up on the rest of the site ("aesthetics and laser practices") with "med spa" handled in the hero subhead and throughout the body.

## Page structure recommendation

Page length: 1,800 to 2,400 words. The aesthetics buyer reads quickly and bounces fast, so the page needs to hit hard early and not bury the proof. The Smooth Laser Center case study should be high on the page, not buried in Section 3.

**H1:** Web design for aesthetics and laser practices

(Keyword placement: primary phrase used naturally. The sitewide category language is "aesthetics and laser," so the H1 matches it.)

**Hero subhead:** Custom websites for med spas, laser clinics, and aesthetic injectors. Built to rank locally, look like the practice you actually run, and bring in the right patient before they ever walk in.

(Keyword placement: "med spa," "laser clinic," "aesthetic injectors," and "rank locally" all appear naturally. "Right patient" is the sitewide messaging anchor, kept consistent.)

**Section 1, the proof (moved to the top, this is the differentiator):**

H2: How Smooth Laser Center went from invisible online to booked from search

This is unique to this page. On the other two industry pages, proof lives in Section 3. Here, it leads. The aesthetics buyer is the most skeptical of generic pitches, and the strongest single move available is to put real proof above the fold (well, above the second scroll). One paragraph summary of Smooth Laser Center's outcome (Mona Perez, Washington Township NJ, women's wellness laser practice that went from minimal Google presence to consistent search-driven bookings). Link to the full case study. Use a quote from Mona's existing testimonial as a pull quote in the design layer.

This section should be 150 to 200 words. Tight. The full case study lives at its own URL. This section is the trailer, not the movie.

**Section 2, the problem:**

H2: Why most med spa websites look the same

Body covers the pattern: same gold-and-marble color palette, same hero video of a woman touching her face, same boilerplate "luxury experience" copy, same templated treatment pages with stock photography. The reason it happens (most agencies hand med spas off to junior designers using the same brand archetypes, the "Lover/Sage" framework that Bethany Works literally markets, and the same Squarespace templates). The cost (the practice can't differentiate locally, so patients pick on Yelp reviews and proximity, and the practice gets squeezed into discounting).

This is also where the page should briefly demonstrate fluency in patient-side search behavior. One paragraph: "And here's the part most agencies skip. Your patients aren't searching 'medical aesthetics provider.' They're searching 'lip filler in [your town]' and 'Botox near [neighborhood].' If your service pages aren't built around those exact searches, you're invisible at the moment they're ready to book." This is the single highest-leverage move on this page. The buyer will read this paragraph and decide whether you actually understand her business.

Keywords to brush against: "med spa website design," "med spa website not getting clients," "med spa Google ranking."

**Section 3, the offer:**

H2: What goes into a med spa website that actually books

Body covers what a Simple Rabbit build includes for this niche, with the aesthetics-specific elements named:

- Custom design that matches the practice's aesthetic (define this term: not a stock luxury template, an actual design system built from the ground up around the practice's brand)
- Strategic copywriting (with mention of treatment-specific service pages, because treatment pages are the most important conversion architecture in aesthetics)
- Local SEO foundations (define "local SEO" the first time it appears: showing up in local Google searches, including the local map pack, when patients in your area search for treatments. Mention Google Business Profile by name because every med spa owner knows what that is.)
- Booking and conversion paths (with mention of common booking system integrations: Jane App, MindBody, Squarespace Scheduling, Vagaro, etc., because the buyer wants to know you've worked with the tools her practice already uses)
- Before-and-after gallery integration (this is specific to aesthetics. Mention HIPAA-aware patient consent considerations briefly, the buyer respects when designers know this exists.)
- Ongoing care plan
- One point of contact (you, Leann)

Length: 400 to 500 words. This section can run longer than the equivalent section on the other industry pages because aesthetics buyers want concrete specs.

Keywords: "med spa web design," "aesthetics website design," "laser clinic website."

**Section 4, the process:**

H2: How a med spa website project actually works

Reuse the three-step process from the Services page (Discovery and planning, Copy/design/build, Launch and care). Adapt the language: mention treatment-page architecture, before-and-after gallery setup, booking system integration, patient consent flows.

Keywords: "med spa website project," "med spa web design timeline."

**Section 5, the FAQ:**

H2: Common questions from med spa and aesthetics practice owners

This is where the page captures most of the long-tail SEO. Each question is a real search.

1. **How is a med spa website different from a regular medical website?** (High-volume, low-competition query.)
2. **Do you handle before-and-after galleries and patient consent for them?** (Aesthetics-specific buyer-trust query.)
3. **Can my site rank for "Botox in [my city]" or "lip filler near me"?** (Local SEO intent. This is the question every aesthetics buyer secretly wants to ask. Answer it directly: yes, that's the point of building treatment-specific service pages, and here's roughly what to expect.)
4. **What about HIPAA, advertising compliance for medical procedures, and FDA/state regulations on before-and-afters?** (Regulatory query that practitioners worry about. Compliance for cosmetic procedure advertising is real, especially around results claims and before-and-after photos. Don't pretend to be a lawyer, but show you know the territory and that the build supports compliance.)
5. **I already have a site I paid an agency a lot of money for, but it's not bringing in clients. Do you do redesigns?** (Captures Stage C buyers. This is a very common situation in aesthetics specifically because so many med spas have already paid an agency $10K-$25K for a site that didn't perform.)
6. **How is this different from working with [Mila Design / ClinicGrower / Aesthetic Brand Marketing / Intrepy]?** (Don't name competitors directly in the FAQ, but answer the implicit question. The differentiator is: one person handles the whole build, the buyer works directly with Leann, the care plan is real and ongoing, the design is genuinely custom rather than a template-with-your-logo, and Simple Rabbit isn't trying to upsell paid ads or AI chatbot funnels on top.)

Each answer 100 to 130 words. This section will run 700 to 800 words total and carry a lot of the long-tail SEO weight on this page.

**Section 6, the CTA:**

H2: Ready when you are

Standard sitewide CTA block, link to contact page. Add a softer secondary CTA to the playbook signup, because the marketing-aware aesthetics buyer often wants to validate her thinking before reaching out, and the playbook is designed for exactly that pre-contact research moment.

## Competitive notes

Top 5 ranking pages and where the openings are:

**1. miladesignco.com (Mila Design Co)**
Strengths: Closest direct competitor to Simple Rabbit in this niche. Female-led, boutique positioning ("first female-founded and female-led boutique branding agency dedicated to medical spas"), focused exclusively on medical spas, takes few projects per year. Very similar market position.
Weaknesses: Heavy on brand-strategy positioning ("Brand Strategy and Web Design") and lighter on SEO depth. The site reads brand-agency-first, web-second. Squarespace-leaning portfolio. Expensive (likely the highest-priced option in this competitive set).
The opening for Simple Rabbit: WordPress + ongoing care plan. Mila does branding and websites; Simple Rabbit does websites built to rank and stay running. The care plan is genuinely the differentiator against Mila Design.

**2. bethanyworks.com (Bethany Works)**
Strengths: Sophisticated content marketing (her "Best Web Designers for Med Spas" post is genuinely well-written and ranks well). Heavy positioning on the "Brandcend methodology" and Jung archetypes.
Weaknesses: The methodology framing is the strength and the weakness, it sounds smart but reads as agency-speak. The buyer who wants something simpler and more direct will bounce.
The opening: voice and directness. Simple Rabbit's brand voice is a clear contrast to the archetype-framework positioning. Lean into that.

**3. intrepy.com (Intrepy)**
Strengths: Award-winning, established healthcare marketing agency, full-service. $7,500 starting price.
Weaknesses: Big agency feel, account managers, multiple handoffs. The buyer who's been burned by an agency reads this site as the kind of place that burned her.
The opening: one-person studio. Leann does the whole build. No account manager, no team handoffs, no upsells.

**4. clinicgrower.com (ClinicGrower)**
Strengths: "#1 Growth Marketing agency for medical and aesthetic practices in America" claim, strong content marketing, big name in the space.
Weaknesses: Reads as a paid-ads-and-funnels agency that also does websites. The buyer who wants design-first and SEO-second over paid acquisition will bounce.
The opening: organic-first positioning. Simple Rabbit builds websites that bring in patients without paid ads. ClinicGrower's whole model assumes you're running paid ads alongside. That's a real philosophical fork.

**5. aestheticbrandmarketing.com (Aesthetic Brand Marketing)**
Strengths: 100% focused on aesthetic practices, plastic surgeons, dermatologists. Founder lectures at industry conferences.
Weaknesses: Positioned for the high-end plastic surgery market, not the independent NP-injector or smaller med spa. Their content reads "for thought-leader plastic surgeons," not "for the practice owner who launched her own injector studio."
The opening: the female-led independent injector practice (which is the fastest-growing segment in aesthetics and the one most poorly served by ABM's positioning).

**One general competitive observation worth saying out loud:** the aesthetics web design SERP is the most "agency-saturated" of the four industries. Every result above is a real business with real budget. None of them are positioned for the specifically-female-led, independent, ongoing-care-included, voice-driven studio that Simple Rabbit is. The opening is real but the differentiator has to be sharp on the page.

## Open questions

These need your input before the page is built.

**1. The Smooth Laser Center case study placement.**
The brief recommends moving proof to Section 1 instead of Section 3, because this buyer is the most skeptical of generic pitches and the case study is the page's strongest single differentiator. Want to confirm that, or do you prefer to keep proof in the standard Section 3 position to maintain consistency with the other industry pages? My take: break the pattern here. Aesthetics is the niche where this move pays off most.

**2. The Mona Perez quote.**
The existing testimonial on the homepage is strong ("Leann was absolutely amazing! Brought my ideas and vision into life..."). Do you want to use that same quote on the industry page, pull a different quote from the case study itself, or get a fresh quote from Mona specifically tied to the search/booking outcomes? My recommendation: get a fresh outcomes-focused quote ("Since the new site, I've been getting bookings from Google every week" or similar). The existing testimonial is great for general trust; an outcomes quote does more work for this specific page.

**3. The Botox-near-me question.**
Question 3 in the FAQ ("Can my site rank for 'Botox in [my city]'?") is the highest-leverage question on the page. The answer needs to be honest and specific. Two options:
- (a) Yes, with caveats. Treatment pages built around hyperlocal long-tail can rank well. Broad "Botox near me" alone is harder.
- (b) Yes, here's the realistic range. Top 5 in 6 to 12 months for hyperlocal treatment queries with the right setup.
My recommendation: (b). The buyer respects specificity. Generic "yes we can rank you" answers read as the agency pitch she's already heard.

**4. Pricing.**
Same question as the previous two pages. Aesthetics builds tend to come in higher than the other industries because the scope is bigger (more service pages, gallery integration, booking system integrations, more design polish). If the build is $10K-$20K, the page should feel premium and unapologetic about price. If it's $6K-$12K, the page can lean more accessible. Either works, but the buyer here is more comfortable with high price tags than the menopause buyer.

**5. The agency-burned buyer.**
Stage C aesthetics buyers very often arrive having paid a previous agency $10K-$25K for a site that didn't deliver. The brief addresses this in Question 5 of the FAQ, but it's worth deciding: do you want a dedicated paragraph somewhere on the page that names this specific situation directly, or is the FAQ-only treatment enough? My recommendation: dedicated paragraph in Section 2 (the problem section), one or two sentences, naming the pattern without bashing competitors. Something like: "If you've already paid an agency more than you'd like to admit for a site that doesn't bring in clients, you're not alone, and you're not the problem." That sentence will land hard with this buyer.
