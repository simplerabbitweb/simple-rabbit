# SEO Brief: Web Design for Functional and Integrative Medicine Practices

## The buyer

A functional or integrative medicine practitioner (most often MD or DO, sometimes NP, PA, or ND) running a private-pay practice. She is most often 40 to 60 (slightly older average than the menopause buyer), with a longer clinical history (often 10 to 25 years in conventional medicine before opening or joining a private-pay practice). She is IFM-certified or actively pursuing it, or trained at an institute like A4M or the Andrew Weil Center for Integrative Medicine. She has a deeper bench of credentials than the average wellness practitioner and she knows it, but she's also tired of being represented online by a website that looks like every other "root cause" practice in the country. Her stage is most often B (recently left insurance, 0 to 18 months in) or C (established but plateaued, 2+ years in). She is searching for a designer who already understands functional medicine and won't need her to explain what IFM is, what root-cause medicine means, or why her appointments are 90 minutes instead of 15.

A practical buyer-profile note: this buyer skews male more than the menopause buyer (still mostly female, but a meaningful share of MDs and DOs in this category are men). The page should not lean as feminine in voice or imagery as the menopause page can. Keep the voice peer-to-peer, not a girlfriend-to-girlfriend register. The buyer respects clinical seriousness above all.

## Primary target keyword

**"web design for functional medicine practice"** (and the closely related "functional medicine website design" and "functional medicine website designer").

Volume note: low to medium. Higher than the menopause equivalent, because the niche is older and more established. This is the right anchor because it captures the most direct buyer intent and it's the phrase practitioners actually use when describing what they need.

Settled vs. educated guess: It's settled that this phrase has real existing competition (Functional Medicine SEO, Yakadanda, Angelique Vestil, Melanie Pylant, Up Designs all rank for some variation of it). It's an educated guess that Simple Rabbit can break in within 6 to 9 months because the differentiator is voice and depth (not raw SEO firepower). The agencies have generic copy. The solo competitors are real, but they're targeting the broader "wellness/functional/naturopathic" umbrella, not the specifically-female-led-private-pay practitioner that Leann is built for.

## Page structure recommendation

Page length: 2,000 to 2,500 words (longer than the menopause page because the buyer reads more carefully and the SERP rewards depth in this niche).

**H1:** Web design for functional and integrative medicine practices

(Keyword placement: primary phrase used naturally in the H1, no stuffing.)

**Hero subhead:** Custom websites for private-pay functional and integrative practices. Built to rank locally, written for the patient who searches for a real root-cause approach.

(Keyword placement: "private-pay functional and integrative practices" and "root-cause approach" both appear naturally. The phrase "rank locally" signals the SEO angle without saying "SEO" yet.)

**Section 1, the problem:**

H2: Why most functional medicine websites look the same

Body covers 250 to 350 words on the pattern: the same stock photos of a woman holding a kale smoothie, the same "treat the root cause, not the symptom" tagline, the same earth-tone color palette, the same pages with the same headings. The reason it happens (most designers don't specialize in this niche, so they reach for the visual shorthand they've seen). The cost (your practice looks indistinguishable from the one a town over, so patients pick on price or proximity, not fit).

Keywords this section should brush against: "functional medicine website design," "functional medicine website not ranking," "why functional medicine websites look the same."

**Section 2, the offer:**

H2: What goes into a functional medicine practice website

Body covers 350 to 450 words on what a Simple Rabbit build actually includes for this niche, with a note on the specifically functional medicine elements (root cause language, longer appointment models, lab work and supplement integration considerations, IFM and certification display, the shift from acute care visuals to long-term partnership visuals). This is where the page anchors on the primary keyword cluster.

Sub-elements to include (not as bullets in copy, but as natural mentions):
- Custom design top to bottom
- Strategic copywriting
- Local SEO foundations (define this term the first time it appears: "showing up in local Google searches when patients in your area search for functional medicine")
- Booking and conversion paths
- Ongoing care plan
- One point of contact (you, Leann)

Keywords: "functional medicine web design," "integrative medicine website design," "WordPress website for functional medicine practice."

**Section 3, the proof:**

H2: Built for practices like yours

This is the section where the proof gap shows up. You don't have a published case study in functional medicine yet. The workaround is a soft pivot:

Use the Smooth Laser Center case study as adjacent women's-wellness proof (one short paragraph, link to the full case study). Then add a paragraph naming your audit-based pattern recognition: you've reviewed dozens of functional and integrative medicine sites, you know the structural patterns that hold practices back, and you know what's working for the practices in this niche that are ranking and converting. Don't claim case studies you don't have. Don't manufacture metrics.

Add a small sentence at the end of the section: "A functional medicine case study is in development. If you'd like a heads-up when it's published, the newsletter is the place." This converts the proof gap into a soft CTA for the playbook signup.

**Section 4, the process:**

H2: How a functional medicine website project actually works

Reuse the three-step process from the Services page (Discovery and planning, Copy/design/build, Launch and care). Adapt the language to the niche (mention IFM credentials, lab work integration, supplement dispensary considerations, longer patient consultation language, etc.).

Keywords: "functional medicine website project," "functional medicine web design timeline," "WordPress functional medicine site."

**Section 5, the FAQ:**

This is the highest-leverage SEO section because each question targets a real long-tail query.

H2: Common questions from functional medicine practitioners

Questions to include (each one is a real search):

1. **How is a functional medicine website different from a regular medical website?** (Targets a high-volume, low-competition query.)
2. **Do you understand IFM, A4M, root cause, or other functional medicine frameworks?** (Targets buyer-trust queries.)
3. **What about HIPAA compliance, supplement dispensaries, and lab integrations?** (Targets technical/regulatory queries that practitioners worry about.)
4. **Can my site rank for "functional medicine [my city]"?** (Targets local SEO intent. Define "local SEO" the first time it appears.)
5. **I already have a functional medicine website. Do you do redesigns?** (Captures Stage C buyers searching "functional medicine website redesign.")
6. **What if I'm transitioning out of insurance and don't have my practice fully set up yet?** (Captures Stage A buyers, the early-pipeline group.)

Each answer should be 80 to 120 words. This section will likely contribute 600 to 700 words to the page total.

**Section 6, the CTA:**

H2: Ready when you are

Standard CTA block, matched to the sitewide pattern. Link to the contact page. Add a softer secondary CTA to the playbook signup or the newsletter, because the functional medicine buyer often researches for weeks before reaching out.

Names where Squarespace works (early stage, low budget) and where it breaks (SEO depth, custom integrations, scale). This is a high-search-volume comparison query that almost every solo competitor in the space targets, and Simple Rabbit can rank for it with a more honest take than the agencies write.
- Buyer journey: All three stages, this is one of the most-searched comparison queries in the niche.

## Competitive notes

Top 5 ranking pages and where the openings are:

**1. functionalmedicineseo.com (Functional Medicine SEO)**
Strengths: Long-running domain authority, full-service offering, dedicated to the niche. Ranks for many of the queries this page wants.
Weaknesses: Generic agency copy, "we're passionate about helping wellness-focused practices grow," reads as undifferentiated. Treats web design as a sub-service to SEO, not a craft. No named designer or studio leader on the surface (no human face).
The opening for Simple Rabbit: voice and craft. Lead with the studio (Leann), not "the team."

**2. yakadanda.com**
Strengths: 15+ years in functional medicine, name-drops Mark Hyman, Jill Carnahan, Michael Ruscio. Genuine depth in the niche.
Weaknesses: Site reads dated (last meaningful updates 2022). Heavy on technical specs ("4-6 weeks to completion," "SSL/TLS certificates") and light on positioning. The buyer reading it doesn't feel seen.
The opening: modern voice, modern design language, and a clear point of view about who the page is for (specifically the female-led private-pay practice, not the Mark Hyman-tier celebrity doctor).

**3. angeliquevestil.com (Brand With Impact)**
Strengths: This is the closest direct competitor in the niche. 7+ years, solo, niche-focused, very strong content (her blog post on functional medicine web design is genuinely good). She positions on "I get it because I specialize."
Weaknesses: She's not focused on women's wellness specifically (her portfolio includes nutritionists, dietitians, fertility coaches, acupuncturists). She uses "client" not "patient" in places, which signals the broader umbrella.
The opening: niche down further. "Women's wellness practitioners" is a tighter focus than "health, wellness, and healthcare practitioners." Simple Rabbit can be the studio for the specifically-private-pay-female-led-medical-practice subgroup, where Brand With Impact is broader.

**4. melaniepylantagency.com (Melanie Pylant)**
Strengths: Solo, naturopathic and functional focus, copywriter-first which gives her good voice. Squarespace specialist.
Weaknesses: Squarespace-only, which is a real ceiling for the buyer who wants WordPress (and SEO depth). Her page positions on "modern Squarespace websites that look fabulous," which is a different buyer than yours.
The opening: WordPress is the right platform for SEO-serious practices, and Simple Rabbit can own the "WordPress for functional medicine" position cleanly because most solo competitors are Squarespace-locked.

**5. up-designs.co (Up Designs / Kate Newnham)**
Strengths: Niche-focused, branded around "Launch in 21 days," personal positioning (Kate worked at a functional medicine practice, was accepted to Penn State's Master's in Integrative Health).
Weaknesses: The 21-day promise reads as time-pressured and templated, which conflicts with the "custom" positioning the buyer wants. Heavy emphasis on "high-conversion" language reads salesy in this niche.
The opening: longer, more considered builds. The 6 to 10 week Simple Rabbit timeline is actually a positioning advantage for the practitioner who wants the build done right, not done fast.

## Open questions

These need your input before the page is built.

**1. The proof gap.**
Same flag as the menopause page. You don't have a published case study in functional medicine. The brief works around it with adjacent proof and audit pattern recognition. My recommendation is the same: ship it. Holding the page costs you 6 to 9 months of ranking time in a more competitive niche, and that's the niche where ranking lag matters most.

**2. The buyer skew.**
This page's buyer is on average older, more credentialed, and slightly less female-skewed than the menopause buyer. Should the page be written to fully reflect that, or should we keep the female-led tone consistent with the rest of the Simple Rabbit site? My recommendation: keep the female-led positioning (it's the studio's actual specialty and the source of most referrals) but adjust the voice register on this page to be slightly more clinically-serious, less girlfriend-to-girlfriend. Use "practitioner" more than "she" in the writing, even if Simple Rabbit's actual client mostly will be a woman.

**3. The IFM/A4M angle.**
Naming specific functional medicine training credentials on the page (IFM Certified Practitioner, A4M, Andrew Weil Center, Kresser Institute) is high-leverage proof for the buyer who has those credentials. It shows you know the landscape. The risk is overclaiming familiarity if you don't actually know the difference between, say, IFM-trained and IFM-certified. Want me to write up a quick functional medicine training landscape primer so the page can name credentials accurately, or would you rather the page stay credential-agnostic?

**4. The Squarespace question.**
The functional medicine buyer is more likely than the menopause buyer to already be on Squarespace and asking whether she should switch. The page should address the WordPress vs. Squarespace question directly somewhere (probably in the FAQ). Should the answer be opinionated ("we build on WordPress because it's better for SEO at scale") or neutral ("we build on WordPress, here's why, but Squarespace can be the right call in some cases")? My recommendation is opinionated. The buyer values clinical clarity and respects a designer with a clear point of view. Hedging reads as weak.

**5. The pricing positioning.**
Same question as the menopause page, just to inform the tone. Functional and integrative medicine builds tend to come in slightly higher than the menopause builds because the scope is bigger (more service pages, more conditions, more lab and supplement integrations). If the build for this niche is in the $8K-$15K range, the page should feel premium and unhurried. If it's $5K-$10K, the page can lean a little more accessible. Either works, but they read differently.
