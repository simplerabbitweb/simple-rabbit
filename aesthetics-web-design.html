The Problem

Why most med spa websites look the same.

The pattern in med spa website design is easy to spot. Gold and marble color palette. Hero video of a woman touching her cheekbone. Tagline about a “luxury experience” or “results-driven care.” Treatment pages with stock photography and copy lifted from manufacturer websites. Same layout, same language, different logo.

It happens because most agencies that serve aesthetics practices work from the same brand archetypes and the same templates. The luxury aesthetic shorthand is a real design category, and it’s what designers reach for when they don’t know the practice well enough to build something specific. The result is a site that communicates “med spa” the way a chain communicates “coffee.” Technically correct. Completely forgettable.

The cost is real. When your site looks like every other laser clinic in the area, patients can’t differentiate on substance. They pick on Yelp reviews, proximity, and whoever is running a discount this month. That pushes the practice into competing on price when the actual differentiator is expertise and trust.

And here’s the part most agencies skip. Your patients aren’t searching “medical aesthetics provider.” They’re searching “lip filler in [your town]” and “Botox near [neighborhood].” If your service pages aren’t built around those exact searches, you’re invisible at the moment they’re ready to book. Med spa website not getting clients is usually a search architecture problem before it’s anything else. The Build

What goes into a med spa website that actually books.

Every build starts with copy. Before any design work begins, I write what each page says. For a med spa or laser practice, that means individual treatment-specific service pages: standalone pages for Botox, filler, laser resurfacing, body contouring, or whatever the practice actually offers. Not one long services page with everything stacked on it. Separate pages, each one written around how a patient in your market searches for that specific treatment. Treatment pages are the most important conversion architecture in aesthetics, and most med spa sites get them wrong.

The design is built from the ground up around the practice’s brand. That phrase gets used loosely, so here’s what it means: no templates, no shared design systems licensed across multiple clients, no stock luxury palettes. Colors, type, layout, and imagery direction are built from scratch for this practice. The site looks like yours because it was made for yours.

Local SEO foundations are built in from the start. Local SEO is the work that puts your practice in front of patients in your area when they search for treatments on Google, including in the local map pack at the top of “near me” results. That means location-accurate pages, a well-aligned Google Business Profile, and title tags written around what patients actually type, not what the practice wants to rank for.

Booking integrations are handled at the build stage. Most aesthetics practices run Jane App, MindBody, Vagaro, or Squarespace Scheduling. Whatever your system, the booking flow is woven into the site rather than added as an afterthought.

Before-and-after galleries are built to work: fast load times, mobile display, organized by treatment, and structured to support your patient consent process. Aesthetics patients use results photos as proof more than any other content type. The gallery needs to earn that attention.

After launch, ongoing care keeps the site current. New treatment pages, gallery updates, seasonal promotions, hosting and security. One point of contact: me, Leann. No account coordinators, no junior designers, no explaining your brand to someone new six months in. If your practice also offers hormone optimization or wellness services alongside aesthetics, the hormone and menopause care page covers how that side is handled.

Common Questions

Common questions from med spa and aesthetics practice owners.

How is a med spa website different from a regular medical website?

The patient makes a different kind of decision. A med spa patient has usually already decided she wants the treatment. She knows roughly what it costs and she’s choosing between providers, not deciding whether the treatment is worth doing. That means the site isn’t trying to sell her on the treatment. It’s trying to sell her on this practice over the three others she just looked at. The copy, the design, the social proof, and the before-and-after gallery all work toward that one decision. A regular medical website is trying to establish trust with a patient who may not know what she needs yet. Different goals, different architecture.

Do you handle before-and-after galleries and patient consent for them?

Yes. Before-and-after photos are often the most-viewed content on an aesthetics website, and they need to work: fast load times, mobile-optimized display, organized by treatment, and built to support your patient consent process for the use of images in marketing. The gallery is built into the site rather than loaded from a third-party platform that slows load times and breaks the design. The consent process lives with your practice, but the site is structured to support it. I’m not a HIPAA attorney, but I know the compliance territory well enough to build the site correctly and flag the right questions during planning.

Can my site rank for “Botox in [my city]” or “lip filler near me”?

That’s the point of building individual treatment pages. When each treatment has its own page, written around the search terms patients use in your market, the site gives search engines something specific to index for each one. A single services page listing every treatment can’t compete with a standalone page built for that search. The timeline depends on your market and competition. You won’t rank overnight. But the practices that consistently show up for “Botox in [city]” and “lip filler near me” have specific, well-structured treatment pages. The ones that don’t, usually don’t show up at all.

What about HIPAA, advertising compliance, and regulations on before-and-after photos?

HIPAA for a marketing website typically centers on how patient data is collected and stored, which lives with your booking system and intake forms rather than the site. The bigger compliance territory in aesthetics is advertising regulation: the FTC’s rules around before-and-after imagery, results-may-vary disclosures, and misleading outcomes claims. Some states have additional rules around how cosmetic procedures can be advertised. I’m not an attorney and won’t give legal advice on this, but I know the territory well enough to build the site in a way that supports your compliance approach and to flag the questions worth running by your counsel during planning.

I already have a site I paid a lot of money for, but it’s not bringing in clients. Do you do redesigns?

Yes, and this is one of the most common situations I see in aesthetics. Practices that paid a significant amount to a med spa agency, got a site that looks polished, and then watched it do nothing for organic search. The problem is usually that the site was designed as a brand piece, not a search and conversion tool. Beautiful design, wrong architecture: one services page instead of individual treatment pages, no local signals, a Google Business Profile that doesn’t match the site. A redesign addresses all of that. Same process as a new build, same timeline, same outcome: a site that earns its keep.

A few things stand out. With a larger agency, you work through account managers and get handed between project roles. With Simple Rabbit, you work directly with Leann from the first call through the last update. The design is genuinely custom, not a licensed template with your logo placed in it. The care plan is ongoing rather than a contract that ends at launch. And the scope stays clean: web design, copywriting, local SEO foundations. Not paid ads, not social media management, not AI chatbot funnels bolted on top. If you want those things, there are specialists who do them well. Simple Rabbit focuses on the channel that compounds over time without a monthly ad spend.
