Why Most Menopause and Perimenopause Practice Websites Don’t Rank Locally (and What to Fix First)

By Leann Frank · May 6, 2026 · 8 min read
Photo by Unsplash

Most menopause and perimenopause practice websites were built for a patient who already knows the practitioner’s name. They were not built for the woman in the next town over who just heard about HRT for the first time and typed “perimenopause specialist near me” into Google last night.

That gap is the whole problem.

The practices I see in this niche have something in common: a referral pipeline that worked for years, a website that was never really meant to do heavy lifting, and a quiet sense that the patients should be finding them by now. Some are. Most aren’t. The ones who are searching for help with hot flashes, sleep that fell apart at 47, brain fog, low libido, or labs that keep coming back “normal” are landing on a competitor three towns away who happens to have a website Google can read.

This isn’t about your clinical work. Your clinical work is the easy part. It’s about whether your website is built in a way that lets a search engine introduce you to the patient who’s already looking for you.

Here’s why most menopause and perimenopause practice websites don’t rank, and the order I’d fix it in.

The website was built for the patient who already knows you

Walk through a typical menopause practice site. You’ll usually find a homepage that says “welcome,” an about page that lists credentials, a services overview that talks about “personalized care” and “a whole-woman approach,” and a contact form.

That site is a fine handoff for a patient who got your name from her best friend. She’s already sold. She’s confirming you exist and looking for the phone number.

It’s almost useless for a stranger. A 48-year-old woman who’s been told her thyroid is “fine” and her hormones are “in range” is not searching for “personalized care.” She’s searching for the symptom that’s been wrecking her life since January. If your site doesn’t say the symptom out loud, on a page Google can index as being about that symptom, you don’t show up.

The fix isn’t a redesign. It’s structure. The website needs to be rebuilt around what patients actually type into Google, not what felt right to say at launch.

There are no real service pages

This is the single biggest reason menopause practices don’t rank locally. Almost every site I audit in this niche has one Services page that lists everything: bioidentical hormone therapy, perimenopause workups, weight loss, IV therapy, peptides, GLP-1s, sometimes aesthetics, sometimes thyroid, sometimes adrenal support. All on one page. Often as a bulleted list under a single H1.

Google can’t rank one page for ten different things. That’s not how it works.

Each service needs its own dedicated page. One page for bioidentical hormone replacement therapy. One page for perimenopause consultations. One page for the GLP-1 program. One page for testosterone therapy in women, if that’s a service. Each page focused on one search, with the search terms in the page title, the H1, the URL, and the body copy. Each page answering the questions the patient searching for that specific thing actually has.

The patient who’s googling “bioidentical hormone replacement therapy [your town]” is a different person, in a different mindset, with different questions, than the patient googling “GLP-1 program [your town].” If both of them land on the same generic services page, neither one feels seen, and Google has no clean signal about what your site is actually about.

This one change moves the needle more than almost any other. A practice with eight individual service pages built correctly will outrank a practice with one services overview, every time.

The language is clinical, the searches aren’t

Menopause and perimenopause have an unusual language gap. Practitioners write in clinical terms. Patients search in symptom terms. The words don’t match.

You write “hormone optimization.” She searches “why am I waking up at 3am.” You write “bioidentical hormone replacement therapy.” She searches “estrogen patch near me” or “do I need progesterone if I had a hysterectomy.” You write “comprehensive perimenopause workup.” She searches “test for perimenopause” or “perimenopause specialist that takes me seriously.”

The clinical language isn’t wrong. It signals that you know what you’re doing. But if it’s the only language on the site, your service pages match almost none of the actual searches happening in your area. The fix is to use both. Clinical terms for credibility, plain-language symptom terms for search.

A perimenopause page that mentions sleep disruption, mood changes, anxiety that arrived out of nowhere, periods that stopped making sense, and labs that came back normal will rank for searches the same page wouldn’t rank for if it only said “comprehensive hormonal evaluation.”

The site has no local schema

This one’s invisible to the buyer, which is why it gets skipped. Schema markup is structured code in the back of your site that tells Google exactly what kind of business you are, where you’re located, what services you offer, and who the practitioner is. It’s the difference between Google guessing and Google knowing.

Most menopause practice sites have no schema at all. A few have basic LocalBusiness schema. Almost none have MedicalBusiness schema, individual Service schema for each treatment, Person schema for the practitioner, or FAQ schema on the pages that have FAQs.

Schema is also one of the main signals AI tools like ChatGPT and Google AI Overviews use to decide who to recommend when a patient asks for a perimenopause specialist in your area. I wrote about that here if you want to go deeper. The short version: a site without schema is hard for both Google and AI to read accurately, and that’s a ranking penalty in both places.

The Google Business Profile is set up but abandoned

The Google Business Profile is the highest-leverage piece of local visibility you have, and it’s the one almost every practice in this niche treats as a one-time setup task.

The profile gets created, the address goes in, “doctor” gets selected as the category, and then nothing. No services listed individually. No FAQ. No posts. No new photos in eighteen months. No responses to reviews. No mention of menopause or perimenopause anywhere on the profile, even though that’s the entire specialty.

An incomplete GBP loses to a complete one in the local map pack consistently. The patient searching “menopause doctor near me” sees three businesses on the map, and the practice that filled in everything (services with descriptions, a real FAQ, recent photos, attributes like “women-owned” and “telehealth available,” posts from the last 90 days) wins the click.

If you do nothing else this quarter, fix the GBP. Here’s what to look at field by field.

What to fix, in what order

You can’t do everything at once. Here’s the order that gets the most return for the time spent.

1. Fix the Google Business Profile this week

It takes about four hours done properly. Add every service individually with its own description. Set the right primary category (Women’s Health Clinic, Functional Medicine Doctor, or Hormone Specialist depending on your specialty, not just “Doctor”). Add secondary categories. Fill in the FAQ with real patient questions. Upload recent photos of the practice. Turn on every attribute that applies. Write a Google Post once a month going forward.

This single fix has moved more rankings in my client work than almost any other change. It’s also the only one you can do without touching the website.

2. Build out the service pages over the next month

One page per service. Real pages, not anchor links on a single Services page. Start with the three services that bring in the most revenue or the most patients you actually want. For most menopause and perimenopause practices, that’s:

  • Bioidentical hormone replacement therapy (or whatever you call your HRT program)
  • The perimenopause consultation or workup
  • The next biggest revenue driver, often GLP-1, testosterone therapy in women, or thyroid

Each page needs the service name and your city in the page title, an H1 that matches what patients search, body copy that uses both clinical and symptom language, the questions patients actually ask, and a clear next step. Add the rest of the service pages over the following two months.

3. Rewrite the homepage so it names the patient and the symptom

The first screen of the homepage should answer three questions in the time it takes a patient to scroll once: what specifically you treat, who you treat it for, and why you’re different from a conventional provider. “Welcome to our practice” answers none of these. “Functional menopause and perimenopause care for women whose labs keep coming back normal” answers all three.

4. Add schema markup to every page

This usually requires a developer or a properly built website, which is part of why most practices skip it. MedicalBusiness schema on the homepage. Service schema on each service page. Person schema for the practitioner. FAQ schema where you have FAQs. Done once, it works in the background forever.

5. Then start writing

Once the structure is right, blog posts compound. Posts that answer the specific questions patients in your area are searching: what to expect at a first menopause appointment, how perimenopause testing actually works, what’s the difference between bioidentical and synthetic hormones, what the new research on HRT and breast cancer actually says. Each post is another ranked page, another way Google introduces you to a patient who didn’t know your name yesterday.

Writing without the structure underneath it is what most practices do, and it’s why their blogs don’t move the needle. Structure first, content second. That order matters.

Most menopause and perimenopause practices have a referral pipeline that’s enough to keep them busy, and a website that’s coasting on it. That works until it doesn’t, usually around the time a competitor with a better site starts showing up on page one for searches you should own.

The fix isn’t more marketing. It’s a website that’s built to be found, in the order that produces results.
