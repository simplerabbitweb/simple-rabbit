5 signs your practice website is costing you patients

By Leann Frank · March 28, 2026 · 6 min read

Most practice website problems are invisible to the provider but obvious to the patient. The person who searched your name after a colleague mentioned you and never reached out. The inquiry that went quiet after you shared your fees. The intake call that started with "do you take insurance?" before you had said a single word. None of these feel like website problems in the moment. They feel like bad luck, or a slow season, or just the way things go. But often they trace back to the same source.

Your website is either doing the work of positioning your practice, building patient trust, and filtering for the right inquiries, or it is quietly working against you. Here are five signs it is doing the latter.

Sign 1: Your intake calls feel like the first impression

If you get on a call and find yourself re-explaining who you are, what you treat, who you work with, and why your approach is different from a conventional practice, your website did not do its job. A well-built site does that work before the call ever happens. By the time someone books an initial consultation, they should already understand your model, your approach, approximately what the investment involves, and what makes your practice the right fit. The call is for connection and specifics, not basics.

When every intake call feels like starting from zero, the site is not pre-qualifying or pre-educating anything. Patients arrive with very little context about your model, which puts all the explanation pressure on you every time.

Sign 2: Warm referrals go quiet after they look you up

A referral from another provider or a satisfied patient arrives with built-in trust. Someone who knows your work and thinks highly enough of you to pass your name along has already done the hard part. That trust should be easy to hold on to. But if the website they land on does not match the version of you the referral source described, the trust erodes fast.

This happens more than most providers realize. A practitioner is doing excellent work, building a strong reputation, and earning real referrals. But the site still looks like an earlier chapter of her practice, or still reads like a conventional insurance-based office, or says very little about the specific approach that makes her different. The referral goes warm, then lukewarm, then nothing. The site created doubt where there should have been confirmation.

If warm referrals are not converting the way you would expect, ask whether your site matches the reputation you have built. Read more about what that gap is actually costing you.

Sign 3: Every inquiry leads with price or insurance

When the first question in every inquiry is "how much do you charge" or "do you take insurance," it is a sign the site did not do enough work to establish the value of your model before the conversation started. These questions are not just about budget. They are a signal that the patient did not find enough on your site to feel oriented toward what you offer.

A site that communicates your approach clearly, explains how private-pay functional medicine works, and shows the outcome of working with you creates a different kind of inquiry. Patients arrive already understanding the model. They ask about your availability, your specific approach to their condition, what the first visit involves. Price still comes up, but it comes up in context rather than as the opening move.

The difference is not just in the design. It is in the specificity. A site that speaks directly to the right patient, using language that reflects what they are actually searching for and worrying about, filters out the wrong inquiries and draws in the people who are already oriented toward what you do. For more on what that looks like, see the five things private-pay patients look for before booking.

Sign 4: You are attracting patients who are not a good fit for your model

This one is subtle. Inquiries are coming in, but they are consistently from patients who expect insurance reimbursement, who push back on your fee structure, or who are not genuinely ready to invest in the functional medicine approach. The volume is there but the conversion rate is low, and the intake conversations are draining.

A website signals who it is for. The language, the positioning, what is emphasized, what is explained, what is left vague, all of it communicates something about who belongs here and who does not. A site that reads like a general wellness practice draws general wellness patients. A site that clearly positions your functional medicine or women's health specialty, explains the model, and speaks directly to the patient who has already been through conventional care without answers draws that patient specifically.

If you are consistently attracting the wrong inquiries, the site is probably calibrated to a broader or vaguer version of your practice than what you actually run.

Sign 5: You have not updated it since you transitioned to private-pay

This is the most common and most overlooked problem for providers who made the shift from insurance to direct pay. The fees changed. The model changed. The patient profile changed. And the website stayed the same. Still written like a conventional practice. Still implying you accept referrals from any provider. Still not explaining what a private-pay functional medicine visit actually looks like.

The gap this creates is real. A patient reads the site, forms an impression of what this practice is, and then speaks to you or receives your fees and finds a mismatch. That disconnect does not always end the conversation, but it creates friction that would not have been there if the site had kept pace with your practice. It makes patients feel surprised instead of prepared.

Your website should reflect the practice you are actually running. If your model has changed, your positioning has sharpened, or your ideal patient has shifted, the site needs to follow. Here is how to structure a site that converts the patients you want.

What to do if you recognize any of these

You do not necessarily need to rebuild everything from scratch. But the starting point is the same: look at your site the way a potential patient would, someone who does not already know you, landing there for the first time with a health problem they have not been able to solve through conventional care. What does that site tell them? Does it make reaching out feel like an obvious next step?

If the answer is no, or even maybe, that is worth taking seriously. The patients you are not hearing from are not leaving feedback. They are just leaving.
