Why So Many Private Practices Are Suddenly Asking Me About Email

Something has shifted in the last few months. The requests I'm getting from private-pay practices used to be almost entirely about websites and getting found on Google, and a lot of them still are. But more and more of them now start somewhere else: "Can you help me with my email?"

Sometimes it's email templates, sometimes a welcome sequence for new patients. Often it's a way to follow up with the people who fill out a form and then go quiet, or a monthly note that doesn't sound like it came from a hospital marketing department.

I think the instinct behind those requests is exactly right, and it's worth explaining why.

The math most practices never run

The people on your email list already raised their hand. They found you, read enough to trust you a little, and gave you their address. That is a warmer audience than anyone you'll reach through an ad or a search result, and most practices do almost nothing with it.

A patient who's been with you for two years and a prospective patient who downloaded your guide last week are at completely different points. They have one thing in common, though: a short, well-written email is the cheapest, most direct way to stay in front of both of them. No algorithm decides who sees it. No ad spend involved. It just lands in the inbox of someone who already knows your name.

That's why I built email into the framework I use with every practice, not as an afterthought but as two of its four pillars.

Where email actually lives in the ACRR system

I organize patient growth around four stages: Attract, Convert, Retain, Refer. Email does real work in two of them.

Convert. Plenty of people find a practice, get interested, and then nothing happens. They meant to book, and life got in the way. A simple follow-up sequence (two or three short emails after someone shows interest) catches a meaningful share of the people who would have quietly drifted off. These are people who already looked at you and liked what they saw. They just needed a reason to come back this week instead of someday.

Retain. This is the pillar practices underuse the most. Established patients are the easiest people in the world to lose track of, because everything is going fine until it isn't. A patient who hasn't heard from you in eight months is a patient who's a little more likely to drift, a little more likely to forget what made your care different in the first place. A short, genuinely useful note every few weeks keeps the relationship warm. It reminds people why they chose a practice like yours, the kind that thinks in root causes and long arcs instead of fifteen-minute slots.

A note on scope: this is marketing email. Educational notes, re-engagement, the occasional update about what you're doing in the practice. Clinical communication lives in the patient portal, and nothing in this work touches protected information. The line is firm, and I keep all of my work on the marketing side of it.

If you've been thinking about this

The fact that this keeps coming up tells me a lot of practices are sitting on a warm list and a quiet inbox at the same time. If that's you, the most useful starting point is figuring out where the leaks actually are across your whole patient acquisition picture, because email is usually one piece of a bigger pattern.

That's what my ACRR Patient Acquisition Checklist is for. It's a short self-assessment that scores how ready your practice is for steady, predictable patient growth, and it'll show you fairly quickly whether email is your weak spot or just one of several. You can work through it on your own, and if you want to talk through what it surfaces, you can book a call from there.

Either way, if patients and prospective patients keep slipping through the gaps between visits, your list is probably the least expensive place to start closing them.
