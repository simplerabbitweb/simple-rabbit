The Problem

Why most fertility practice websites lose the patient before the first call.

The patient researching fertility care is not casually browsing. She has probably been thinking about this for months. She has read the studies, joined the forums, and compared providers across three tabs. When she lands on your website, she is not looking for a welcome message and a phone number. She is looking for evidence that you understand what she is actually going through, and that you are specific enough to be trusted with it. Most fertility practice websites are not built for her.

The first problem is tone. A patient who has been disappointed before, by a diagnosis, a cycle, a provider who didn’t listen, is not going to respond to a site that leads with bright stock photography and optimistic language about “your family-building goals.” That kind of copy reads as generic at best and dismissive at worst. The emotional stakes in fertility care are among the highest in any private-pay niche. The design and the words have to reflect that.

The second problem is surface-level content. A well-researched patient can tell within thirty seconds whether a site was written for her or for the algorithm. If the copy is thin, if the service pages don’t distinguish between preconception support and active fertility treatment and egg freezing and IVF coordination, she will conclude that the practice doesn’t understand the differences either. She moves on to someone whose site does.

The third problem is invisibility. Naturopathic fertility doctors, integrative reproductive endocrinologists, IVF support specialists, these are real searches patients type. If the site isn’t structured to capture them, you are not in the conversation at all. Local SEO for fertility practices requires specific attention to how patients actually describe what they’re looking for, and most fertility sites don’t have those foundations in place.

The Build

What a website built for a fertility practice actually contains.

Every project starts with the copy. Before a single page is laid out, I write what the site says. For a fertility practice, that means thinking carefully about which patients you serve, what stage they’re in when they find you, and how to speak to each of them honestly. A patient considering preconception support is in a very different place than one who has already been through an IVF cycle. The site has to hold both, and be clear about who you help and how.

The design follows the copy. Nothing is built from a template. The visual tone for a fertility practice should feel hopeful and grounded, warm enough to signal care, structured enough to signal expertise. It is a careful balance, and it requires designing from scratch rather than adapting something that started somewhere else.

After launch, the site stays current. Ongoing care means new service pages when your offerings change, content updates as the practice grows, and hosting and security handled in the background. You focus on your patients. The site keeps up.

The Background

Why fertility sites need a different approach than general medical sites.

Simple Rabbit works exclusively with private-pay women’s wellness practices. That focus matters for fertility specifically, because fertility care sits at the intersection of several things that general medical web designers rarely handle well: high emotional stakes, a deeply-researching patient, multiple distinct service types that require separate content strategies, and a private-pay model that relies on trust rather than insurance referrals to bring patients in the door.

A general medical website template does not know the difference between a naturopathic fertility doctor and an integrative reproductive endocrinologist, or why it matters to the patient choosing between them. It does not know how to write service pages that speak to a woman who is three months into preconception preparation versus one who is actively trying after a loss. Fertility sites require that level of specificity, in the content architecture, in the copy, and in the tone, to work.

I’ve audited dozens of private-pay women’s wellness sites, including fertility and preconception practices. The gaps show up consistently: content that treats IVF support and preconception care as the same thing, copy that leads with optimism before establishing credibility, no local search structure for the specific terms patients use when they have a specific need. These are fixable problems, but they require someone who understands the niche to fix them.

Common Questions

Common questions from fertility and preconception practice owners.

How do I write about fertility treatment without sounding salesy?

The short answer is: lead with what is true, not what is hopeful. Salesy copy in fertility care usually comes from the impulse to reassure, to tell a patient everything is going to work out, before you know anything about her situation. The patient who has been researching for months does not trust that. What she trusts is specificity, honesty about what you can and cannot offer, and copy that acknowledges the complexity of what she is navigating without making it heavier than it needs to be. That balance requires care in the writing, but it is achievable, and it is exactly what I work through during the copy phase of every fertility project.

Should I have separate pages for preconception care, IVF support, and egg freezing?

Almost always yes, and for two reasons that reinforce each other. First, the patients are different. A woman preparing her body before trying has different questions than a woman coordinating with a reproductive endocrinologist for an IVF cycle, and both are different from someone considering egg freezing as a standalone decision. Combining them on one page means you are writing for no one in particular. Second, the searches are different. “Preconception care specialist,” “IVF support naturopath,” and “egg freezing consultation” are distinct queries with distinct intent. Separate pages let the site compete for each one. During planning we look at your specific services and your local market before finalizing the page structure.

How do I reach patients who are months into their research?

The patient months into her research has already filtered out the surface-level sites. What keeps her on a page is depth: service pages that explain what your approach actually involves, an about page that makes your training and philosophy legible, and copy that uses the correct terminology without being inaccessible. She is also looking for proof that you have thought carefully about what she is navigating, that the site reflects genuine understanding, not just optimized keywords. Content that goes deep enough to be genuinely useful is how you earn her attention at that stage. That is the standard I write toward for every fertility project.

My patients are in a vulnerable emotional state, how does the site handle that?

With care, and with intention. Designing for emotional vulnerability in fertility care means two things above all: the site should never feel like it is selling something to a woman who is in pain, and it should never pretend that outcomes are more certain than they are. In practice, that shapes every copywriting decision, the language on the homepage, the way services are described, how the contact page is written, even the visual weight of the design. Warmth matters, but so does steadiness. A patient who has been through disappointment needs to feel that the practice understands the weight of what she is considering, not that the website is trying to cheer her up.

Will my fertility practice site rank for local searches?

What I can promise is that the foundations will be right. That means pages written around terms your patients actually search, “naturopathic fertility doctor near me,” “IVF support specialist [city],” “integrative reproductive endocrinologist”, accurate location signals throughout the site, and a Google Business Profile that aligns with what the site says. Whether you reach the top spot depends on your market, your competition, and factors outside any web designer’s control. But the site will be structured to give search engines something substantive to work with. For a full breakdown of what local SEO foundations look like for private-pay practices, this article covers the specifics.

Do I need to address the cost of fertility treatments on the website?

Not in the sense of publishing a price list, but you do need to address the private-pay model honestly. For fertility care especially, patients are often navigating significant costs across multiple providers, and a site that avoids the subject entirely can feel evasive. What works well is a clear statement of how your practice operates: that you are private-pay, what that means for the patient relationship, and what the process for getting started looks like. This positions the investment as a meaningful decision rather than a surprise, and it filters for patients who are genuinely ready to commit to the kind of care you provide. I work through that framing during the copy phase.
