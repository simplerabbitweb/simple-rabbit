How to Get More Google Reviews

Google reviews are one of the first things a potential patient or client sees. Here’s a simple system for getting them consistently.

Step 1: Get your Google review link

You need a direct link that takes someone straight to the review form, no searching, no hunting for your profile. Here’s how to get it:

  1. Go to business.google.com and sign in
  2. In the left menu, click Ask for reviews
  3. Copy the short link Google generates for you

Save this link somewhere easy to access. You’ll be sharing it by text and email on a regular basis.

Step 2: Ask right after a good appointment

The best time to ask is in the moment, right after an appointment where the patient or client was clearly happy, or after a meaningful milestone in your work together. Not three months later in a mass email.

You don’t need a polished script. Just be direct:

“I’m really glad we were able to [help you with X]. If you have a minute, I’d love a Google review. It makes a big difference for people searching for a doctor like me. I’ll text you the link.”

Most patients or clients will say yes if you ask in person first. Getting verbal agreement before sending the link dramatically improves follow-through.

Step 3: Follow up with a text or email

Send the link the same day, or within 24 hours while the visit is still fresh. The longer you wait, the less likely they are to do it.

Text example:

Hi [Name], great seeing you today. Here’s that Google review link if you get a chance (takes about a minute). Thank you! [your link]

Email subject: “A quick favor from Dr. [Last Name]”

Hi [Name],

It was really great seeing you [today / this week]. If you have a moment, I’d appreciate a Google review. It helps other patients or clients who are looking for what we offer find their way to us.

Here’s the link: [your link]

No pressure either way. I just wanted to ask. Thank you!

Step 4: Put a QR code in your office

Print a small sign or tent card with a QR code that links directly to your review page. Place it at checkout or in your waiting area so patients or clients can scan it before they leave.

You can generate a free QR code at qr-code-generator.com. Just paste in your Google review link. Print it, frame it, and you’re done.

Step 5: Respond to every review

Google rewards practices that respond, and potential patients read your responses when deciding whether to book. That said, because your practice is covered by HIPAA, how you respond matters a lot. See the section below for the full breakdown, but the short version: keep responses warm, professional, and generic. Don’t confirm anyone is a patient or reference their visit or care in any way.

Safe response for a positive review:

“Thank you so much for taking the time to share your experience. We truly appreciate your kind words and are so glad you had a positive visit with us.”

Safe response for a negative review:

“Thank you for sharing your feedback. We take all concerns seriously and would love the opportunity to address this directly. Please reach out to our office at [phone/email] so we can connect.”

Both are warm, professional, and HIPAA-safe. More on why that matters below.

HIPAA and online reviews: what your practice needs to know

Most concierge, functional, and precision medicine practices are covered entities under the HIPAA Privacy Rule. There are specific rules around online reviews that every practice owner should understand before anyone on your team starts responding publicly.

Asking for reviews is fine. HIPAA does not prohibit you from asking patients to leave a review. The risk is not in the ask. It’s in the response.

The rule that trips most practices up

When a patient leaves a Google review, they are choosing to share their own information. That’s their right. But when you respond to that review, any response that acknowledges the reviewer as a patient becomes your disclosure, and HIPAA governs what you can disclose publicly.

This means the following are all potential HIPAA violations in a public review response:

  • Saying “Thank you for visiting our office” (you just confirmed they’re a patient)
  • Thanking someone by first name in a way that confirms the relationship
  • Referencing their visit, treatment, condition, or anything about their care
  • Responding to a negative review with any specifics about what happened

Here’s the part that surprises people: even if a reviewer posts their full name, their diagnosis, and every detail of their care, your response still cannot confirm any of it. The rule applies regardless of what the patient chose to share publicly.

Real consequences

The HHS Office for Civil Rights has pursued enforcement actions specifically over review responses. A North Carolina dental practice was fined $50,000 for disclosing a patient’s name, visit dates, treatment plans, and clinical details in a Google review response. Manasa Health Center, a New Jersey psychiatric practice, paid $30,000 and entered a corrective action plan after disclosing patients’ mental health diagnoses in Google review responses. These are not hypothetical risks.

What HIPAA-safe response templates look like

The key is that your response should not confirm the reviewer is a patient, reference their visit, or include any detail specific to their experience. Generic is good here.

Positive review:

“Thank you so much for taking the time to share your experience. We truly appreciate your kind words and are so glad you had a positive visit with us.”

Negative review:

“Thank you for sharing your feedback. We take all concerns seriously and would love the opportunity to address this directly. Please reach out to our office at [phone/email] so we can connect.”

The follow-up text and email

Sending a message that says “great seeing you today” is low risk but worth knowing: it implicitly confirms someone visited your practice. If you want to be conservative, keep your ask neutral: “Hi [Name], I wanted to share a quick link. We’d love a Google review if you have a moment: [link].”

Sharing or reposting a patient’s review

A patient writing their own review is their choice. You screenshotting that review and posting it to your website, Instagram, or anywhere else is your disclosure, and that requires the patient’s written authorization. Don’t amplify patient reviews without it.

A note on your staff

If anyone on your team manages your Google Business Profile or responds to reviews on your behalf, they need to know these rules. A well-meaning front desk person thanking a reviewer by name can create a compliance issue even if you never see it. Keep approved response templates somewhere easy to find, and make sure anyone with access to your profile has been briefed.

This section is a general overview, not legal advice. For specific situations or to review your current process, your own attorney or HIPAA compliance officer should be your final word.

A few other things to avoid

  • ✗Don’t offer incentives (discounts, gifts, free visits) in exchange for reviews. Google prohibits it and can remove your listing.
  • ✗Don’t ask for reviews in bulk all at once. A sudden spike looks suspicious and may trigger a Google filter.
  • ✗Don’t have staff or family write reviews. Fake reviews can get your profile penalized.

That’s really all there is to it. Ask after every good visit, make it easy with a link, and the reviews will build on their own over time.
