# Simple Rabbit — Pain Points and Messaging

## The Six Core Pain Points

Every consulting engagement, blog post, social caption, and email should map back to at least one of these. They are listed in order of how often they surface in sales conversations.

---

### Pain 1: Inconsistent patient flow

**What she says:** "Some months I'm full. Other months I'm chasing."

**What's actually happening:** She has no repeatable patient acquisition system. New patients come from referrals and word-of-mouth, which are unpredictable. She has no content working for her, no search visibility, no email list doing anything useful.

**The cost she feels:** Anxiety about the months where the calendar thins out. Can't plan, can't hire, can't raise prices with confidence because she doesn't know if the growth is real or a lucky streak.

**How to address it in copy:**
Lead with the feast-or-famine dynamic. She knows this feeling. Then connect consistent patient flow to having a system, not just luck. The ACRR framework is the system.

---

### Pain 2: Wrong-fit patients calling

**What she says:** "I keep getting patients who push back on my prices, or don't follow through on the protocol."

**What's actually happening:** Her website and content are attracting a general audience instead of her specific right-fit patient. The messaging is too broad, too clinical, or modeled after an insurance-based practice's language.

**The cost she feels:** Time wasted on discovery calls that go nowhere. Energy spent managing patients who aren't compliant or who leave after one visit. The sense that the work she does most is the work that matters least.

**How to address it in copy:**
The right website attracts and pre-qualifies. It speaks directly to the patient who already believes in root-cause medicine and is ready to invest. The wrong website says "welcome everyone" and gets exactly that.

---

### Pain 3: Invisible on Google

**What she says:** "I know people are searching for what I do, but I don't come up."

**What's actually happening:** No local SEO setup, no Google Business Profile optimization, no content targeting the search terms her ideal patient uses. A competitor with a worse practice and a better-optimized website is getting her patients.

**The cost she feels:** Direct revenue loss. She's built a good practice and it's not findable. Patients who would be a perfect fit are booking someone else.

**How to address it in copy:**
Be specific. Not "SEO is important." "The practitioner across town who charges less than you is showing up when someone searches 'functional medicine [city]' because her website is optimized and yours isn't." That specificity earns trust.

---

### Pain 4: The website looks generic

**What she says:** "It looks like every other functional medicine website. Stock photos, vague copy, nothing that says anything about me or what I actually do."

**What's actually happening:** The site was built from a template (or with a designer who didn't understand the niche), so it looks like a hundred others. Nothing differentiates. The right patient can't tell why she should choose this practice over the next one.

**The cost she feels:** She's embarrassed to send people to the site. She knows it's the first impression and it's not her. She keeps meaning to fix it and keeps not having time.

**How to address it in copy:**
Specificity = credibility. A site that mentions she specializes in PCOS and perimenopause in women 35–55 in Bergen County NJ is doing more work than a site that says "comprehensive, integrative, personalized care." Generic is invisible.

---

### Pain 5: Can't update it and nothing gets maintained

**What she says:** "My designer built it but I don't know how to change anything. The last time I tried I broke it."

**What's actually happening:** She's on a platform she doesn't understand (often a custom WordPress with no training, or a locked-down Wix/Squarespace that's more limited than it looks). Nothing gets updated. The blog is from 2021. The team bio has a person who left two years ago.

**The cost she feels:** The site is actively working against her. Stale content signals to Google that the site isn't active. Wrong information confuses or loses potential patients.

**How to address it in copy:**
The care plan solves this at the root. She shouldn't be in her own CMS unless she wants to be. If she wants to update it herself, the system is built so she can. If she doesn't, the system is built so she never has to.

---

### Pain 6: Technical problems become her problems

**What she says:** "Last month the contact form stopped working and I didn't find out for three weeks. Before that, the site was down for a day and I have no idea why."

**What's actually happening:** No ongoing site monitoring, no proactive maintenance, no one watching. Plugin conflicts, expired SSL certificates, domain renewals that fall through the cracks.

**The cost she feels:** Patient inquiries that never arrive. Emergency tech support situations she has to solve at 11 PM. The nagging awareness that the site could go down and she wouldn't know.

**How to address it in copy:**
This is the "one person to email" promise. The care plan means someone is watching so she doesn't have to be. Frame it as insurance with active benefits, not just a backup.

### The Problem Section

Most websites built for medical practices have one thing in common: they were built for the doctor, not for the patient.

They're full of clinical credentials and vague reassurances. They don't say who the practice is for. They don't show up when a patient searches for exactly what the doctor does. And they don't make anyone feel like they've found the right place.

So the right patients scroll past. The wrong ones call. The doctor ends up explaining herself on every discovery call. And the website keeps sitting there, doing nothing.

---

### The Solution Section

A website that actually works for a private-pay practice does three things:

1. **Shows up.** It ranks for the searches your ideal patient is actually doing.
2. **Says the right thing.** The copy speaks to the specific patient you want, not every patient who might theoretically be interested.
3. **Runs without your involvement.** You update it when you want to. Otherwise, it handles itself.

That's what Simple Rabbit builds. Custom WordPress sites, built from scratch, with local SEO and a care plan from day one.

Use for subheads, email opening paragraphs, social bios.

- "Custom websites and ongoing care for private-pay practices that want the right patient to find them."
- "A website built specifically for your practice, with the technical side handled for as long as it's online."

Use for About pages, long emails, blog post intros, sales call scripts. Connects buyer's current stage to the offer, and the offer to the life she's building.

## Hard Rules for All Copy

- "cash-pay" is banned. Always use "private-pay."
- No em-dashes anywhere.
- No triple adjective lists ("clean, intentional, and strategic").
- No "Not just X, but Y" constructions.
- No medical advice, no specific patient outcome claims.
- No false urgency ("only 3 spots left," "booking out fast").
- No competitor names. Punch at the category, not specific people or agencies.
- Never "we" when "I" is more honest. Leann is the studio.
- No coach-speak, spa-speak, or guru-speak. See voice-guideline.md for the full banned words list.
