## Email 1 (Day 1): What the Best Practice Homepages Have in Common

**Subject:** Three things the best private-pay homepages do

**Body:**

Hi,

I've audited a lot of practice websites. The ones that consistently bring in the right patient share three traits. Not design traits. Strategic ones.

**They name the patient before they name the practitioner.**

The homepage doesn't open with "welcome to my practice, I specialize in..." It opens with the patient. "For women in perimenopause who've been told their labs look fine." "For practitioners who left insurance and need their website to match the new positioning." The reader sees herself first, the practitioner second.

**They make a specific claim, then prove it on the same screen.**

Not "comprehensive women's health" (vague) but "private-pay functional medicine for women 35-60" (specific). And on the same screen as the claim, there's a piece of proof: a testimonial, a result, a named credential. The patient lands, sees the claim, sees the proof, and trusts the page in the first 5 seconds.

**They make the next step obvious without being pushy.**

There's a clear button. There's a phone number visible. There's a contact form that doesn't feel like a sales form. The patient who's ready can move forward in one click. The patient who's not ready can keep reading.

Now test your own:

Open your homepage in an incognito window so you see what a new patient sees. Set a timer for 5 seconds. Look at it. Then close your eyes and answer:

1. Who is this for?
2. What does this practice actually do?
3. How do I take the next step?

If you couldn't answer all three, your homepage is leaking. Not because it's ugly. Because it's vague.

The fix is almost always specificity, not redesign.

Tomorrow we look at whether patients can find you in the first place.

Leann

## Email 2 (Day 2): Are Patients Actually Finding You?

**Subject:** Open Google in incognito and try this

**Body:**

Hi,

A patient who hasn't found you yet is searching one of two ways. She's typing your specialty plus "near me." Or she's typing your specialty plus your city. The practices showing up in the top 3 of those searches are getting the calls. Everyone else is invisible.

**The audit:**

Open Google in an incognito window. Sign out of your account if you're signed in. Then search the way a new patient would.

Try these:

- "[your specialty] near me" (do this from your office, or use a VPN to fake your location)
- "[your specialty] [your city]"
- "[the specific service you most want to be known for] [your city]"

For each search, are you in the top 3 organic results? Are you in the local map pack at the top? Are you on page 2 (which is functionally invisible)?

**If you're not showing up, the answer is almost always your Google Business Profile.**

Your GBP is the single highest-leverage marketing asset for a local practice. It's also the one most practitioners set up once and never touch again. A complete GBP outranks an incomplete one in the local map pack consistently. The patients who would find you through "near me" searches are finding the practice down the street instead.

I built a free PDF that walks through the 8 GBP fields most practitioners ignore, with screenshots and exact instructions. It takes about 30 minutes to work through and noticeably moves your local visibility.

If you searched today and didn't find yourself, that's the gap.

## Email 3 (Day 3): What You Can't See

**Subject:** The pieces you can't audit yourself

**Body:**

Hi,

The first two days were things you could audit yourself. Today is the list you can't.

These are the things that quietly affect patient acquisition but don't show up when you look at your own site. You'd need either a developer's tools or someone who audits practice websites for a living to see them.

**The list:**

- **Page speed.** A practice site that takes more than 3 seconds to load loses a measurable percentage of visitors. Most do.
- **Mobile experience.** Not "is it mobile responsive" (almost all sites are now). The real question: is the patient experience as good on a phone as on a desktop? Usually it isn't.
- **Schema markup.** This is the structured code that tells Google "this is a medical practice, this is the address, these are the services." Sites without it don't show up in the rich results that get the clicks.
- **AI readability.** Patients are increasingly using AI tools (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google's AI summary) to find practitioners. Your site needs to be readable to AI, not just to humans. Most practice sites are not.
- **Conversion paths.** This is harder than it sounds. It's not about adding more buttons. It's about making sure the right patient ends up on the right page at the right moment.

This is the deep-dive on the Google Business Profile. It lives as a downloadable PDF, not an email, so it doesn't slow down the course but rewards the subscriber who wants to go deeper.

### Title
**The Google Business Profile Audit: 8 Fields Most Practitioners Ignore**

### Subtitle
A 30-minute walkthrough to fix the highest-leverage marketing asset for a local practice.

### Structure (one page per field, 8 pages plus intro and closing = 10 pages total)

Each field gets:
- The field name and where to find it
- What most practitioners do (the failure mode)
- What complete looks like
- Why this specific field affects local ranking or patient experience
- One screenshot example

The 8 fields:

1. **Categories.** Primary plus secondary. The right ones, not the closest ones.
2. **Hours.** Including holiday hours. Up to date this month.
3. **Services.** Each service listed individually with a description, not a generic blurb.
4. **FAQ.** Questions you actually get asked. With real answers.
5. **Photos.** Recent ones. Of the actual practice, not stock images.
6. **Reviews and review responses.** Are you responding to reviews? All of them, not just the bad ones?
7. **Posts.** The "what's new" feature. When did you last post one?
8. **Attributes.** The yes/no checkboxes for things like "women-owned," "telehealth available," "wheelchair accessible."

Closing page: a soft CTA to the website service for practitioners whose audit revealed bigger problems than the GBP.

## Implementation notes

**Email cadence:**
The course can run on Flodesk or any email service that supports automated sequences. Set the welcome email to send immediately. Set Day 1 to send within a few minutes of welcome. Days 2 and 3 send 24 hours apart.

After Day 3, the subscriber automatically rolls into the regular newsletter list (the Hutch). The course ends, the relationship continues. Don't make them sign up for the newsletter separately.

**Subject lines:**
Each subject is short and concrete. Easy to test if open rates wobble. Swap the subject before swapping the content.

**Day 1 timing:**
Day 1 should land within minutes of signup, not the next morning. People sign up for a lead magnet because they want help right now. If you delay Day 1, the open rate drops noticeably.

**The pitch on Day 3:**
Day 3 is the only email with a direct CTA to the contact form. The pitch is structured to earn it: the email opens with value (the technical pieces she can't audit herself), then transitions explicitly with "this is the part where I tell you what I do, because it's relevant." That sentence is doing real work. It signals self-awareness about the pitch and gives her permission to read it as part of the value, not as a separate sales pitch.

**The bonus PDF:**
Until the PDF exists, link Day 2's "Download the GBP audit" button to a placeholder page that says "PDF coming this month, your email is on the list to receive it the moment it's ready." This is honest, sets expectation, and turns a delay into a small relationship-building moment.

## What changed from the previous version (and why)

If you read the v1 of this course, here's what's different and the reasoning:

**Renamed from "The Patient Acquisition Self-Audit" to "The Private-Pay Practice Audit."**
The new name puts buyer identification first. Someone who clicks "private-pay practice audit" is already telling you what they are. That's a stronger signup filter than topical interest, and it filters out non-buyers (practitioners still on insurance, non-healthcare service businesses, agencies, etc.) before they even open Day 1.

**Collapsed from 5 emails to 3.**
The 5-email version had 4 days of value before the pitch. The 3-email version compresses to 2 days of value before the pitch on Day 3. The math is tighter but the course is more likely to be finished. Completion rate matters more than email count for a lead magnet, because uncompleted courses don't convert.

**Day 1 leads with aspiration, not diagnosis.**
The original Day 1 opened with "the 3-second test" which is diagnostic ("here's what's broken"). The new Day 1 opens with "here's what the homepages of practices doing this well have in common," then pivots to self-testing. Aspirational openings put the buyer in curious mode. Diagnostic openings put her in defensive mode. Curious converts better.

**Day 2 merges Google search + Google Business Profile.**
The 8-field GBP deep-dive is too valuable to drop, so it became a downloadable PDF inside Day 2 for the subscribers who want to go deeper. This keeps the course tight without losing the depth.

**Day 3 is the pitch, paired with explicit framing.**
The transition sentence ("This is the part where I tell you what I do, because it's relevant") is intentional. It signals self-awareness about the pitch, which makes the pitch feel less manipulative. The buyer reads it as part of the value, not as a separate sales pitch tacked on.

**The framework you built becomes infrastructure beyond the lead magnet.**
The full audit content (now compressed in the lead magnet) is also available as the spine of:
- Pre-discovery-call screen-recorded audits (warm prospect closer)
- Speaking topic for podcasts and IG Lives (cold audience builder)
- Long-form content for the Hutch newsletter

You don't lose the audit work. You just don't make subscribers do all of it.
