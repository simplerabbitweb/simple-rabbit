## Email 1 (Day 1): What the Best Practice Homepages Have in Common

**Subject:** Three things the best private-pay homepages do

**Body:**

Hi,

I've audited a lot of practice websites. The ones that consistently bring in the right patient share three traits. Not design traits. Strategic ones.

**They name the patient before they name the practitioner.**

The homepage doesn't open with "welcome to my practice, I specialize in..." It opens with the patient. "For women in perimenopause who've been told their labs look fine." "For practitioners who left insurance and need their website to match the new positioning." The reader sees herself first, the practitioner second.

This is the single biggest difference between homepages that bring in the right patient and homepages that don't. And it's almost always a copy fix, not a design fix.

**They make a specific claim, then prove it on the same screen.**

Not "comprehensive women's health" (vague) but "private-pay functional medicine for women 35-60" (specific). And on the same screen as the claim, there's a piece of proof: a testimonial, a result, a named credential. The patient lands, sees the claim, sees the proof, and trusts the page in the first 5 seconds.

Most practice homepages have the claim but bury the proof three scroll-lengths down. The two need to land together.

**They make the next step obvious without being pushy.**

There's a clear button. There's a phone number visible. There's a contact form that doesn't feel like a sales form. The patient who's ready can move forward in one click. The patient who's not ready can keep reading.

Now test your own.

Open your homepage in an incognito window so you see what a new patient sees. Set a timer for 5 seconds. Look at it. Then close your eyes and answer:

1. Who is this for?
2. What does this practice actually do?
3. How do I take the next step?

If you couldn't answer all three, your homepage is leaking. Not because it's ugly. Because it's vague.

The fix is almost always specificity, not redesign. Rewriting the first 50 words of your homepage to land all three of those answers will move the needle more than a redesign will.

## Email 2 (Day 2): Are Patients Actually Finding You?

**Subject:** Open Google in incognito and try this

**Body:**

Hi,

A patient who hasn't found you yet is searching one of two ways. She's typing your specialty plus "near me." Or she's typing your specialty plus your city. The practices showing up in the top 3 of those searches are getting the calls. Everyone else is invisible.

**The audit:**

Open Google in an incognito window. Sign out of your account if you're signed in. Then search the way a new patient would.

Try these:

- "[your specialty] near me" (do this from your office, or use a VPN to fake your location)
- "[your specialty] [your city]"
- "[the specific service you most want to be known for] [your city]"

For each search, ask yourself three things:

1. Are you in the top 3 organic results?
2. Are you in the local map pack at the top?
3. Are you on page 2 (which is functionally invisible)?

**Why this matters more than you think:**

Most practitioners overestimate how much of their patient flow comes from referrals and word of mouth, and underestimate how much comes from search. Even patients who hear about you from a friend usually google you before booking. If they google your name and the search results are sparse, that's a credibility hit before the first call.

The other thing patients are searching for is the specialty itself. A perimenopause patient who just heard about hormone replacement therapy on a podcast is going to google "perimenopause specialist [her town]" and pick from whoever shows up. If you're not on page 1, you're not in her decision set.

**Two things to look at if you're not showing up:**

First, is your site actually targeting the right keywords? Look at your homepage and your top service page. Do the words on those pages match what a patient would search? "Functional medicine in [your city]" needs to actually appear in your copy, your headings, and your page titles. Most practice sites describe what they do in clinical language ("integrative protocols for hormonal optimization") but patients search in plain language ("functional medicine doctor near me"). The mismatch is invisible until you go looking for it.

Second, is your site built in a way Google can read? Most practice websites have decorative SEO (a meta description, maybe a few keywords). That's not the same as structural SEO, which is how the site is built: schema markup, local entity setup, page architecture, content that ranks because Google can index it cleanly. Decorative SEO is something you can add to a finished site. Structural SEO has to be built in from the start.

If you searched today and didn't find yourself, that's the gap.

## Email 3 (Day 3): Your Google Business Profile

**Subject:** The 8 fields most practitioners ignore

**Body:**

Hi,

If you only fix one thing this week, fix this. Your Google Business Profile is the single highest-leverage marketing asset for a local practice. It's also the one most practitioners set up once and never touch again.

The audit:

Log into your Google Business Profile at business.google.com. Score yourself on these 8 fields. Honest scoring. Half-credit for "I did this once but haven't updated it."

**1. Categories.** Primary plus secondary. The right ones, not the closest ones.

Most practitioners pick "doctor" and call it done. That's a miss. The closer the category match to your actual specialty, the better Google can rank you. Look for "Functional Medicine Doctor," "Holistic Medicine Practitioner," "Aesthetic Medicine," etc. Add up to 9 secondary categories that match your services. If you do menopause-focused care, "Women's Health Clinic" should be on the list.

**2. Hours.** Including holiday hours. Up to date this month.

Patients trust hours. If they show up at a closed door because your GBP says you're open, you've lost them and probably gotten a 1-star review. Update holiday hours every quarter.

**3. Services.** Each service listed individually with its own description.

Don't just list "consultation" and "treatment." List every specific service you offer ("hormone replacement therapy consultation," "comprehensive perimenopause workup," "GLP-1 program intake") with a 2-3 sentence description for each. Google indexes these. Patients searching for the specific service find you.

**4. FAQ.** Questions you actually get asked. With real answers.

Use the exact wording patients use, not the wording you wish they used. "How much does it cost?" "Do you take insurance?" "What's your wait time for new patients?" Real answers, written like a person typed them. The FAQ field shows up in search results, and it's a chance to qualify the patient before she calls.

**5. Photos.** Recent ones. Of the actual practice, not stock images.

Photos taken in the last 6 months perform better than older photos. Include the exterior of your building (so patients can find you), the interior (so they know what to expect), and one of you (so it feels personal). Avoid stock photography. Patients can tell.

**6. Reviews and review responses.** Are you responding to reviews? All of them, not just the bad ones?

Responding to every review (5-star and 1-star) is a Google ranking signal and a trust signal for patients reading the reviews. Reply within 7 days. Keep responses short and specific. Thank patients by name when you can.

**7. Posts.** The "what's new" feature. When did you last post one?

GBP Posts are short updates that appear on your profile and in search results. Most practices ignore this feature entirely. Post once a month at minimum. Announcements, articles you've written, new services. It signals to Google that the profile is active.

**8. Attributes.** The yes/no checkboxes for things like "women-owned," "telehealth available," "wheelchair accessible."

These are quick toggles in your dashboard. Patients filter searches by attributes. "Women-owned" is a meaningful search filter for women's wellness specifically. Spend 5 minutes turning on every attribute that applies.

**The bigger picture:**

A complete GBP outranks an incomplete one in the local map pack consistently. The patients who would find you through "near me" searches are finding the practice down the street instead. The fix isn't hard, but it's tedious. About 4 hours to do properly the first time. Then 30 minutes a month to maintain.

This single fix has moved more practice rankings in my client work than almost any other change. If you do nothing else this week, do this.

## Email 4 (Day 4): The Patient's First Click

**Subject:** What happens after she lands on your site

**Body:**

Hi,

A patient finds your site. She's interested. She wants to take the next step. Now what?

The audit:

Open your homepage. Imagine you're a patient who just found you for the first time. Walk through the path.

- Can you book an appointment without leaving the homepage? How many clicks?
- Can you call? Is the phone number visible without scrolling?
- Can you message or email? Is there a contact form, or just a "contact us" link in the menu?
- Now scroll to a service page. Same questions.
- Now scroll to the about page. Same questions.

**The pattern most practice websites have:**

Contact information lives on the contact page. Everywhere else, the patient has to go find it. That's a leak. The patient who's ready to book on Tuesday afternoon doesn't want to navigate three pages to do it.

The fix is what I call "every page is a contact page." Every page on the site has at least one path to take action: a booking button, a phone number, a contact form, even a sticky bar at the top with the phone number. The patient should never have to think "okay, where do I go to actually do this?"

This isn't a design preference. It's a conversion principle. The further away the action lives from the patient's eye, the fewer patients take it.

**The other pattern that quietly hurts:**

Many practice websites have multiple calls to action that compete with each other. "Book now" next to "Schedule a consultation" next to "Contact us" next to "Send a message." The patient sees four options and doesn't know which one is the real next step.

Pick one primary action and use it consistently across the site. If you want patients to book directly, "Book now" everywhere. If you want them to start with a free 15-minute call, "Schedule your free consultation" everywhere. Multiple competing CTAs split attention. One clear CTA, repeated, performs better.

**A specific test:**

On your service pages, can a patient go from "I want to book this service" to "I'm on the booking calendar" without ever leaving the service page? If yes, your conversion path is strong. If she has to go to a separate booking page, lose context, and re-orient herself, you're losing patients to the friction.

**One more thing worth checking:**

Your contact form, if you have one. How many fields does it ask for? More than 5 is too many. Email and message are mandatory. Name is fine. Phone is fine. Anything beyond that needs to earn its slot. The longer the form, the lower the completion rate. A patient who's interested but tired is the most common visitor to your contact form. Don't make her work for the privilege of contacting you.

## Email 5 (Day 5): What You Can't See

**Subject:** The pieces you can't audit yourself

**Body:**

Hi,

The first 4 days were things you could audit yourself. Today is the list you can't.

These are the things that quietly affect patient acquisition but don't show up when you look at your own site. You'd need either a developer's tools or someone who audits practice websites for a living to see them.

**The list:**

**Page speed.** A practice site that takes more than 3 seconds to load loses a measurable percentage of visitors. Most do. The reasons are usually invisible: oversized images, plugins that haven't been optimized, hosting that's underpowered. You can run your site through Google's PageSpeed Insights tool to get a number, but fixing the underlying issues usually takes a developer.

**Mobile experience.** Not "is it mobile responsive" (almost all sites are now). The real question: is the patient experience as good on a phone as on a desktop? Usually it isn't. Buttons that are too small to tap. Forms that don't work properly. Navigation that hides important pages. More than 60% of practice website traffic comes from phones. If the mobile experience is worse than the desktop one, that's the majority of your patients struggling silently.

**Schema markup.** This is the structured code that tells Google "this is a medical practice, this is the address, these are the services, this is the practitioner's name." Sites without it don't show up in the rich results that get the clicks. Schema is invisible on the front of the site. You can check whether your site has it, but adding it requires touching the code.

**AI readability.** Patients are increasingly using AI tools (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google's AI summary) to find practitioners. "Who's a good functional medicine doctor in Bergen County?" is now a real question patients ask AI. Your site needs to be readable to AI, not just to humans. Most practice sites are not. The fix involves how content is structured, what headings are used, how services are described.

**Conversion paths.** This is harder than it sounds. It's not about adding more buttons. It's about making sure the right patient ends up on the right page at the right moment. A patient who lands on a blog post about menopause should have a different next step than a patient who lands on the homepage. Most practice sites send everyone to the same generic "Contact" page regardless of where they came from.

I won't walk you through fixing all of these in an email. They take real work and they're hard to do well as a solo practitioner.

## Implementation notes

**Email cadence:**
The course can run on Flodesk or any email service that supports automated sequences. Set the welcome email to send immediately. Set Day 1 to send within a few minutes of welcome. Days 2 through 5 send 24 hours apart.

After Day 5, the subscriber automatically rolls into the regular newsletter list (the Hutch). The course ends, the relationship continues. Don't make them sign up for the newsletter separately.

**Subject lines:**
Each subject is short and concrete. Easy to test if open rates wobble. Swap the subject before swapping the content.

**Day 1 timing:**
Day 1 should land within minutes of signup, not the next morning. People sign up for a lead magnet because they want help right now. If you delay Day 1, the open rate drops noticeably.

**The pitch on Day 5:**
Day 5 is the only email with a direct CTA to the contact form. The pitch is structured to earn it: 4 days of pure value, then on Day 5 the email opens with value (the technical pieces she can't audit herself), then transitions explicitly with "this is the part where I tell you what I do, because it's relevant." That sentence is doing real work. It signals self-awareness about the pitch and gives her permission to read it as part of the value, not as a separate sales pitch.

**Why 5 emails, not 3:**
The 5-email format gives each topic real room to breathe. Each email teaches something specific instead of pointing at a topic and saying "here, look into this." The depth is what builds the trust that makes Day 5's pitch land. Five touchpoints in a week is also better for relationship-building than three. The buyer opens her inbox 5 times and sees Leann's name. That's the long game.

Each email goes deep enough that a practitioner reading it thinks "she really gets this," instead of "she just gave me a topic and a link." Day 1 leads with aspiration ("here's what the practices doing this well have in common"), which puts the reader in curious mode before pivoting to "now test yours." Aspirational openings convert better than diagnostic openings.

By the end of Day 3, the reader has either fixed her GBP herself (in which case she trusts you) or realized she doesn't want to (in which case she's primed for Day 5's pitch).

Day 5's transition sentence ("this is the part where I tell you what I do, because it's relevant") is the most important line in the whole course. It signals self-awareness about the pitch, which makes the pitch feel less manipulative. The buyer reads it as part of the value, not as a sales pitch tacked on.
