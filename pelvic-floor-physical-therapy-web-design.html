The Problem

Why most pelvic floor PT websites don’t bring in enough patients.

Look at the pelvic floor PT websites in most markets and you’ll find the same pattern: a gentle stock photo, a paragraph about “whole-body wellness,” and a short services list with no real description of what those services actually do. Nothing that names incontinence plainly. Nothing that says “if sex has been painful for two years, this is treatable.” The topic is treated like something to tiptoe around rather than something your patients desperately need someone to address directly.

That framing is a problem because your patients are already embarrassed. They’ve often been dealing with prolapse, leaking, or painful intercourse for months or years before they finally search for help. When they land on a website that won’t say the word “incontinence” without softening it into near-meaninglessness, it confirms their suspicion that this is something shameful, and they click away. The site needed to normalize the topic. It did the opposite.

The second failure is SEO. A patient searching for help with postpartum pelvic floor recovery types things like “postpartum physical therapy near me” or “pelvic floor PT for prolapse.” If your site doesn’t have a page written around those terms, not just a passing mention, but a full page with real copy, search engines have nothing to rank. Most pelvic floor PT sites have one general services page that tries to cover everything and ends up ranking for nothing.

Here’s the good news: the existing websites in this space are weak. That means a practice that does it right, plain language, condition-specific pages, solid local SEO, has a real advantage. The Background

Why pelvic floor PT practices specifically benefit from better web presence.

Simple Rabbit works exclusively with private-pay women’s wellness practices. That focus matters here. Pelvic floor PT sits at an interesting intersection: the need is enormous, the treatment is effective, and insurance mostly refuses to cover enough sessions to see it through. That makes private-pay the reality for most practices, and it means the website has to do the job insurance referrals used to do. Patients have to find you, trust you, and decide to pay out of pocket before they ever walk in the door.

I’ve looked at a lot of pelvic floor PT websites. The competitive landscape in most markets is genuinely weak. Practices doing excellent clinical work have sites that don’t name what they treat, don’t rank for the searches their patients are actually doing, and don’t give a hesitant, embarrassed patient any reason to believe she’s in the right place. That’s not a criticism of the clinicians, it’s just an opportunity. The bar is low enough that a well-built site stands out immediately.

The taboo element is real but workable. Patients searching for help with incontinence or painful sex are already past the hardest part, they’ve admitted to themselves that they need help and they’ve started looking. What they need from your website is confirmation that you’re someone they can talk to about it plainly. That’s a copy and tone problem, and it’s solvable. The site I build for your practice will treat these topics the way a good clinician would: directly, without squeamishness, with the kind of matter-of-fact warmth that makes someone feel like they called the right place.

Common Questions

Common questions from pelvic floor PT practice owners.

How do I write about incontinence and painful sex without it feeling clinical or embarrassing?

The same way a good clinician talks about it: plainly, without hedging, and with enough warmth that the person reading doesn’t feel judged. The goal isn’t to sensationalize or sanitize, it’s to say “this is something a lot of women deal with, and it’s very treatable” in a way that a patient actually believes. That means using the right words (incontinence, not “bladder leakage concerns”; painful sex, not “intimacy discomfort”) while keeping the tone conversational and human. Patients who have been quietly dealing with these issues for years can tell when a website is actually comfortable with the topic. That comfort is exactly what gets them to book.

Should I have separate pages for each condition I treat?

In most cases, yes, and especially for pelvic floor PT practices. A patient searching for help with postpartum diastasis recti is doing a different search than one looking for prolapse treatment or a pelvic floor PT for painful sex. One general services page can’t rank for all of those at once, and it can’t speak to each of those patients the way a dedicated page can. Condition-specific pages also give search engines more to work with. During planning we’ll decide which conditions warrant their own pages based on your practice focus and your local competition.

How do I rank locally for pelvic floor physical therapy?

Local ranking comes from three things working together: pages written around what patients actually type (“pelvic floor PT near me,” “postpartum physical therapy [your city]”), a Google Business Profile that aligns with what the site says, and consistent location signals throughout the site. None of this is a guarantee of a top spot, that depends on your market and competition, but these are the foundations that make ranking possible. Practices without them are essentially invisible to someone who doesn’t already know their name.

My patients are embarrassed to even search for this, how does the site account for that?

By making the first thirty seconds feel safe. A patient who is embarrassed about searching “pelvic floor PT for incontinence” is looking for signals that she’s in the right place and that no one is going to make this harder than it already is. The site does that through language that normalizes the topic immediately, a visual presentation that feels calm rather than clinical, and a booking path that doesn’t ask her to explain herself before she’s ready. The site can’t eliminate embarrassment, but it can make clear from the first click that your practice is a place where this is a regular conversation.

Do I need to explain what pelvic floor physical therapy is on every page?

No, and repeating the same explanation on every page actually hurts more than it helps, both for readability and for search. The homepage and a dedicated “What is pelvic floor PT?” page can carry the education load for patients who are newer to the topic. Condition-specific pages can assume that foundation and speak directly to what the patient is dealing with. This structure respects both the patient who already knows what pelvic floor PT is and the one who’s just figuring it out, without making either one wade through content that isn’t for them.

I’m the only pelvic floor PT in my area, will that help my rankings?

It helps, but it doesn’t do the work on its own. Being the only provider in your area means low competition for local keywords, which is a real advantage, but search engines still need the right foundations to know what to rank you for and where. A site without condition-specific pages, location signals, and a well-aligned Google Business Profile can still be nearly invisible even with no competitors. Being the only pelvic floor PT in your area plus a well-structured site is a strong combination. Being the only one with a generic site is a missed opportunity.
